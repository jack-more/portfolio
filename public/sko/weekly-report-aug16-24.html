The verified floor is what we can prove: an order whose session carried a real TikTok click ID. The platform ceiling adds view-through, cross-device email matches, and return-direct buyers our click tracking can't see. Truth sits between; we optimize and budget off the floor's trend, which is rising — verified orders/day went 7–9 early in the window to 14–19 at the peak.

Cross-pollination context: the affiliate book did 267 orders / $52,983 in the same window; 31 of those (11.6%) also carried hard TikTok click evidence and are counted in full on both sides ($760 commission paid on them). That overlap is the gap between summed channel cards and the deduped total.

So did we lose $3K or make $63K? (read this before quoting either)

Spend was $21,765. Our strictest ledger shows $18,512 back (a $3K loss); TikTok's dashboard shows $84,920 back (a $63K profit). Neither is the P&L. They are answers to two different questions:

  • The verified floor ($18.5K) answers: "which orders can we prove, with a click receipt in hand, came straight from an ad?" It only counts a sale when the ad click and the purchase happen in the same browser session. It misses everyone who saw an ad and came back later.
  • TikTok's ceiling ($84.9K) answers: "how much was bought by people we showed ads to?" It counts people who saw an ad and never clicked, people who clicked on their phone and bought on a laptop, and people who came back days later. It is real influence, measured by a party with an incentive to claim everything.

One customer, three ledgers: the Madison example (order SKO-033249)

This is a real order from this week, with the raw tracking row pulled. Follow it and the whole gap makes sense.

What she actually did. She is a five-time repeat customer. Over the last 30 days she visited the site 13 times — every visit direct: she typed the URL or used a bookmark. No ad link, no search, no email link. On Thursday she browsed at 1 AM, came back at 6 PM, went to checkout, and typed in SUMMER30 — the discount code that runs in our TikTok ads.

What her checkout recorded. No TikTok ad was stamped anywhere on the order. No click ID, no campaign, no ad group — nothing. The only "tracking" on her order was the SUMMER30 code itself. So our ledger, correctly, does not count her as an ad sale. She is organic on our books.

What TikTok recorded. We send TikTok a receipt for every purchase (that is how ad platforms optimize). TikTok took her receipt, matched her email to a person its app had shown our ads to, and counted her purchase as an ad conversion.

What RedTrack recorded. Weeks ago she clicked one of our ads. RedTrack's cookie from that click lives for 30 days, so when she bought on Thursday — from a typed-in URL — RedTrack also credited the ad campaign.

One sale. Three ledgers. Nobody is lying: she really did see our ads, she really did once click one, and she really did walk in the front door on her own and use the code the ads taught her. The disagreement is only about where you draw the credit line. Most of the $66K gap between our floor and TikTok's ceiling is thousands of versions of Madison — repeat buyers and code-typers the ads touched but did not visibly deliver.

Where you draw her matters less than knowing she exists: our floor excludes every Madison (too strict), TikTok includes every possible Madison (too generous), and the truth — how many Madisons would still have bought with no ads running — sits in between and is only truly answerable with a holdout test once spend justifies it.

What is certainly true, regardless of ledger:

  1. The whole business, all sources, runs roughly $150K/week of paid orders against this $21.8K of ad spend — the ads are ~7× covered by total revenue even if you credit them nothing else (exact weekly total being pulled to replace this estimate).
  2. The affiliate book did $53K in the window — and its biggest engine is Ethan, whose face is also our top ad. Ad viewers use creator codes; that revenue lands in the affiliate column, not the ads column, but the ads helped cause it.
  3. The provable click floor nearly tripled per-day inside the window (7–9 orders/day → 14–19) as spend scaled — direct evidence the ads move real orders, not just claimed ones.
  4. When we ran this same account at one-third the spend two weeks ago, total business revenue was materially lower. Spend went up 3×; the floor went up 3×; the business grew. That is the causal story, with the honest caveat that the restock landed the same week.

The one-sentence version for leadership: paid ads provably drove at least $18.5K, plausibly influenced up to $85K, cost $21.8K, and the whole business ran ~7× revenue-to-ad-spend for the week — we budget off the provable floor and its trend, both of which are rising.
