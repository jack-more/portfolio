The system

Five roles, one loop

Basketball history sits in random uploads and dying hard drives. Every player gets one organized home for their footage, and the people who organize it are paid and credited — the model that built Wikipedia, pointed at video.

ArchivistsFans who find footage, cut it into clips, and label everything properly. Their job is making each player’s folders rich.
EditorsFans who pull from those folders and make great mixes. Good folders make good mixes fast.
AIDoes the grunt work at a scale people can’t: proposes the labels, matches every clip to the box score, and builds the edit — cuts, ramps, transitions. It never decides what matters and never alters the footage.
PlayersOwn their archive, get wildly more visible from the mixes, and put up prizes for the work they want done.
FansGet the best history reader in sports — and the ones who fall in love with it become the next archivists.

The economy

One token, two doors

Tokens are compute credits. Buy them, or earn them working the archive. They pay for AI in the editor and they enter raffles — that dual use is what makes them worth money and worth grinding for.

InCash and labor

Token sales. Editors in a hurry buy compute. The margin line.

Daily

B2B contracts. Rights-holders pay to get their own libraries organized.

Contract

Auctions & sponsors. Cash for lots, brands funding prize pools.

Event

Corpus licensing. The labeled archive as a search API and dataset.

Year two
OutCompute and people

AI compute. Real cost per render — the token has a floor under it.

Per use

Archivist pay. Cash for verified work, separate from tokens.

Weekly

Prizes. Funded by players and sponsors, never out of wages.

Monthly

Charity. A fixed share of every auction clears to the player’s cause.

Every lot

Buy the compute, or earn it.

Every token can be earned, so nobody has to pay to take part — that keeps the archive the fastest way in and keeps the draws clean. Paid entry into prize draws is regulated state by state and wants counsel before launch; the earn path is what makes it workable.

Rules

Non-negotiable
Rule 01

AI never touches the footage

It proposes labels, matches box scores, times cuts and builds transitions — all of it on the timeline, none of it on the original. No upscaling, no generated frames, no invented detail. The record stays as it was recorded, which is the only reason it will be worth licensing.

Rule 02

Prizes come from players

The best rewards aren’t cash — they’re a signed ball, a call, a seat at a game, funded by the player whose history you kept. Quiet incentives, from the person you did it for.

Rule 03

Credit is permanent

Every clip carries the name of who saved it, forever — on the site and on every mix it appears in. Like a Wikipedia edit history, but public and proud.

Rule 04

Players own their vaults

The archive is open to explore and build on, but each legend owns their own footage and stories — open-sourced in spirit, theirs on paper.

The league

What they cannot build themselves

The league’s history is its most under-used asset. This builds the thing they can’t: a living, organized, rights-clean archive with a fan workforce that loves maintaining it.

For the leagueDecades of footage finally organized, labeled, and searchable — by people, verified, at a quality money can’t buy.
For the playersRetired legends get bigger every month their archive grows — new fans meeting old greatness daily.
The dataThe labeled archive is also the best basketball training data in existence — an asset that appreciates on its own.
The momentStart with Baron and legends who say yes. When the mixes are everywhere and the archive is undeniable, that’s what Baron raises on — with the league in the room.
